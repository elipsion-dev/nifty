Quick Test or Full Test: which should you take?

The two versions measure the same thing on the same scale. They differ in how many questions you answer, how long that takes, and — as a direct result — how tight the margin around your score is.

The Quick Test asks 20 questions, five from each reasoning type, with a relaxed 12-minute timer. Most people finish in 8–10 minutes. It is the right choice if you are curious, short on time, or want a warm-up before committing to the longer version. Its estimate is honest but coarse: for a typical score the model's margin is about ±11 points, so a result of 112 really means "somewhere around 101–123."

The Full Test asks 60 questions, fifteen from each reasoning type, with a 45-minute timer. Plan on 30–35 minutes of real concentration in one sitting. It includes every item format from the Quick Test plus several that only appear here — figure sequences, number tables, superposition and hidden-rule matrices, mirror-image detection, and multi-step logic puzzles — and it reaches both lower and higher on the difficulty scale. For a typical score the margin narrows to about ±6 points, and the ceiling is higher, so very strong reasoners are less likely to run out of hard questions. The two versions share no questions at all, so taking the Quick Test first and then the Full Test is perfectly fine; you will not see anything twice.

Our recommendation is simple. If you only have a few minutes, take the Quick Test and treat the number as a rough indicator. If you want a figure you would be comfortable repeating to someone — with the usual caveat that no online test is a clinical one — take the Full Test, fresh and undistracted.

Why the longer test is more accurate

Every test score is really a measurement with error around it, and that error shrinks as you add questions. The maths works out to roughly the square root of the question count: three times as many questions cuts the margin by a factor of about 1.7, which is exactly the gap between the ±11 of the Quick Test and the ±6 of the Full Test. Three further things help the longer version. A lucky guess or a careless slip moves a 20-item raw score by five percentage points but a 60-item score by under two, so single flukes matter less. A wider spread of difficulty means fewer people hit the floor or the ceiling, where any test stops discriminating. And a broader mix of formats means that being unusually good or bad at one particular puzzle type — mental rotation, say — distorts the overall picture less.

What this IQ test actually measures

Psychologists generally split intelligence into two broad kinds. Fluid reasoning is your ability to solve a brand-new problem you have never seen before — spotting the rule behind a pattern, holding several conditions in mind at once, and drawing a valid conclusion. Crystallised knowledge is what you have already learned: vocabulary, facts, and procedures. Online tests are best at sampling the fluid side, because fluid problems do not depend on your schooling or background as heavily.

Both versions lean on the item formats that appear across reputable cognitive assessments — matrix completion, number series, verbal analogies, odd-one-out classification, syllogism-style deduction, and mental rotation. Those formats are the shared vocabulary of the field and are not owned by any one publisher. What is protected is the specific wording and artwork of published tests, so none of that is reproduced here. Each question was authored from scratch.

The four reasoning types you will see

Number & series. You are shown a sequence such as 2, 6, 12, 20 and asked what comes next. The skill is inductive: you infer the hidden rule (here, the gaps grow by two each step) and extend it. The Full Test adds interleaved series and small number tables where you must find the rule linking each row. These reward flexible arithmetic thinking rather than memorised maths.

Verbal reasoning. Analogies ("hand is to glove as head is to …") test whether you can identify the relationship between two things and apply it to a new pair. Odd-one-out items check whether you can spot the category that ties several items together and find the one that breaks it.

Logical reasoning. Short deductions ask which conclusion must be true given the premises. Many of them are deliberately tempting: a statement can sound right and still be an invalid inference. The Full Test adds seating and ordering puzzles, truth-teller problems, and mislabelled-box puzzles that need two or three steps of reasoning. This is the part that most resembles everyday careful thinking.

Pattern & spatial. Matrix puzzles present a three-by-three grid with one cell missing; you choose the figure that completes the pattern in both directions. Mental-rotation items ask which option is the same shape simply turned, rather than flipped into a mirror image. The Full Test also uses figure sequences, line-overlay puzzles, and matrices whose rule you must discover rather than being told. This family is the closest thing to a pure fluid-reasoning measure.

How your score is estimated

Unlike most free quizzes, this test does not simply count how many you got right. Every question carries a difficulty weight, and your result comes from an item-response model — the same family of statistics used to score professionally normed instruments. Solving a hard late-test item raises your estimate far more than solving an easy warm-up, a built-in 25% guessing floor accounts for the fact that every question has four options, and the range shown alongside your score is the model's own statistical uncertainty, which honestly widens at very high and very low scores and shrinks as you answer more questions. Because the Quick and Full versions sit on the same difficulty scale, their scores are directly comparable; the Full Test simply comes with a tighter range. You also get a percentile estimate and a breakdown showing how many you answered correctly in each of the four reasoning types.

Be clear-eyed about what that number is. A real IQ score comes from a standardised test administered one-to-one by a trained professional and compared against a large, carefully sampled population. This test does neither. Its score is an unnormed self-assessment estimate — a reasonable indicator of how your pattern-based reasoning is running today, useful for practice and curiosity, but not a diagnosis and not a certified figure to put on a form.

How to get the most accurate result

Find a quiet spot and give the test your full attention. Do not use a calculator, search the answers, or ask anyone for help — every one of those inflates the number and makes the breakdown meaningless. The timers are gentle (12 minutes for the Quick Test, 45 for the Full Test); work steadily rather than racing. If a question stumps you, make your best reasoned guess and move on, because a blank counts the same as a wrong answer and the scoring model already accounts for guessing. Expect the later questions to feel hard — they are supposed to, and missing several of them is entirely normal. Fatigue, stress, and distraction all pull scores down, so a single sitting on a fresh mind is your best measure, and for the Full Test that means setting aside a genuinely free half hour.
